# Queryable 4D World Models from In-the-Wild Monocular Video

*Working draft — abstract and introduction.*

## Abstract

Recent work on 4D world modelling has converged on a two-stage recipe: a feed-forward reconstructor recovers an explicit 4D representation from an input video, and a video diffusion model is then conditioned on renderings of that representation to synthesise the final output. The arrangement has produced strong results on camera-controllable video generation, but its scope is narrow. The only question the system is built to answer is what the scene would look like along a single user-specified trajectory; the rendering interface compresses a rich 4D reconstruction into degraded RGB, depth, and mask images before the generator ever sees it; reconstruction and synthesis cannot be jointly optimised through that interface; and each invocation commits to one trajectory rather than supporting partial or repeated reads.

We argue that this is a mismatch between the framing of the task and what a 4D world model should be. A world model is, by definition, queryable: given a query that names which part of the world is wanted, the model should return that part. Controllable video generation is one such query — dense, sequential, RGB-only — but treating it as the goal hides the structure that makes a world model useful. We therefore reframe 4D world modelling as task-conditioned compression of in-the-wild monocular video into a latent whose spatial and temporal axes remain decoupled and independently indexable. The same compressed latent supports random-access queries over `(camera, time)` and exposes several readout heads — RGB frames, depth, geometry — through which different aspects of the world can be retrieved without generating the full video. Controllable generation reappears as one head along one dense trajectory; partial readout, multi-query consistency, and persistent scene memory across queries follow from the latent rather than from added modules. We build the system on top of NeoVerse, keeping its trained reconstructor, data regime, and diffusion backbone, and replacing only the rendering interface with a learned compression and a cross-attention readout. We show that the resulting representation reproduces NeoVerse's controllable generation as a sanity case, supports geometry readout that the original pipeline cannot, and stays consistent across queries that share a scene but not a trajectory.

## 1. Introduction

A useful model of the visual world should let us ask about it. We should be able to point a virtual camera at a scene, advance time to an arbitrary moment, read the geometry of a single object at a single instant, and return to a place we have visited before and find it still there. The recent wave of 4D world models — NeoVerse, GEN3C, TrajectoryCrafter, ReCamMaster, WorldSplat, among others — has made the first of these capabilities possible at a level of visual fidelity that was unimaginable a year ago. None of them addresses the rest.

The reason is structural. The dominant pipeline factorises the problem into two stages that meet at a fixed interface. A feed-forward reconstructor (VGGT, WorldMirror, or Depth Anything 3) turns the input clip into an explicit 4D representation, typically 4D Gaussian Splatting. The user supplies a target camera trajectory and a set of time stamps, the 4D representation is rendered from those viewpoints to produce a stack of degraded RGB, depth, and mask images, and a video diffusion model conditioned on the renders produces the final clip. The decomposition is appealing and recovers spatiotemporal control almost for free: the rendering step is where control "happens," so the generator never has to learn it. But the interface that buys this convenience also bounds what the system can do. The renders are a low-bandwidth surface that throws away the reconstructor's intermediate features — features that encode geometry, motion, and uncertainty more compactly than any image stack. The interface is non-differentiable in any usable sense, so reconstructor errors propagate without correction and the two stages cannot be jointly optimised. And the interface is single-shot: each invocation commits to one trajectory's worth of RGB frames. There is no place to ask for depth instead of RGB, no place to issue a sparse query at a single `(camera, time)`, and no notion of consistency across queries that share a scene but not a trajectory.

Concurrent work on large autoregressive video world models — Cosmos, V-JEPA, and other systems positioned as "foundation world models" — pursues scale and generality but commits to a different shape. These models collapse time into a sequence dimension and treat the world as something that is rolled out rather than indexed. They cannot answer what the world looks like at time `t` from camera `c` without generating every frame leading up to `t`, because the only addressable operation is autoregressive continuation. They are formidable generative models, but as world *models* they fail the query test for a different reason: time is implicit, not addressable.

We think the gap is in the framing rather than in the engineering. The 4D-reconstruction line recovers space and time *before* generation and confines them to a renderable intermediate. The autoregressive line folds space and time *into* generation and loses them as separate handles. A world model that is genuinely queryable needs space and time to survive as first-class, indexable axes of the representation itself. From this position, "camera-and-time control" is not the goal of a 4D world model; it is the simplest behaviour any 4D world model should exhibit, and demonstrating it alone is not evidence of one.

This work therefore reframes the problem. Rather than building a system that controls a video along a specified `(camera, time)` trajectory, we build a system that compresses in-the-wild monocular video into a latent that is queryable at any `(camera, time)`. The shift moves the contribution from the application level ("better camera control") to the representation level ("a compact 4D representation with random-access semantics"), and several commitments follow from it.

The first commitment is to treat compression as the primitive. The system ingests heterogeneous signals — geometry recovered by a frozen reconstructor, appearance latents from a pretrained video VAE, and language from a text encoder — and compresses them into a single latent that is whatever supports the desired set of readouts at the desired fidelity. Phrasing the model this way gives the contribution an axis that is rare in the controllable-generation literature: a rate–distortion characterisation. We can ask how much fidelity each readout retains as the latent budget shrinks, and we can answer that question without invoking visual quality alone. The framing also absorbs the multi-modal input and the multi-head output naturally, so that the same architecture can be evaluated as a video generator, a depth predictor, or a geometry predictor without surgery.

The second commitment is to keep the spatial and temporal axes of the latent decoupled. A latent that mixes the two into an opaque sequence cannot answer a time-frozen camera sweep or a camera-frozen time scrub, nor can it admit out-of-order queries that share state. We therefore require, at the representational level, positional encodings that factor across `(time, camera, image-plane)`; and at the computational level, modulation and attention patterns that route time-dependent information separately from view-dependent information. The point of these design choices is not the choices themselves — they are unremarkable individually — but the property they collectively guarantee: each axis of the world remains an index, not a hidden variable.

The third commitment is to demote controllable generation from a goal to a head. Once the latent is in place, a video-diffusion decoder reading from it along a dense `(camera_t, time_t)` trajectory recovers the controllable case. The same latent, queried sparsely, returns single frames at arbitrary `(camera, time)` without generating intermediate content. Other heads attached to the same latent return depth or point maps. Issued repeatedly, queries that share a scene must agree on geometry and identity even when they do not share a trajectory. These behaviours — partial readout, random-access, and cross-query consistency — are what separate a queryable world model from a controllable video generator, and they are what the empirical sections of this work focus on.

The path we take is a deliberate revision of our own prior work. NeoVerse, in its released form, instantiates the reconstruct-render-refine pipeline described above. We keep its trained reconstructor, its in-the-wild monocular data regime, and its Wan-2.1 diffusion backbone, and we replace the rendering interface with a cross-attention readout that compresses the reconstructor's intermediate features — intermediate transformer layers and the camera token — into a `(camera, time)`-indexed latent. In a first training stage, the new latent is distilled against the control embedding that the rendering pipeline produces, ensuring that the learned representation is at least as informative as the rendering it replaces. In a second stage, additional readout heads and a multi-query consistency objective are trained jointly with the diffusion loss, forcing the latent to support behaviours that the original pipeline cannot express. The architectural changes are deliberately modest. What reorganises the system is the framing, not the parameter count.

Two observations are worth making in advance, since they govern how the rest of the paper is read. First, the claim is not that explicit 4D representations are wrong. Reconstructors that produce 4D Gaussian Splats or pointmaps remain useful — we use one — and the present work does not propose to replace them. The claim is that the *interface* between reconstruction and generation should not be a fixed renderer, and that opening that interface to compression and querying lets the system do things the rendering interface cannot. Second, the work does not claim to deliver an action-conditioned simulator or a language-driven scene editor. Both are natural extensions of the framing — additional query types over the same compressed latent — and we sketch them in §6, but the present paper is bounded to camera, time, RGB, and geometry, and to the consistency properties that hold across queries of that kind.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. §2 places the framing in the context of 4D reconstruction, controllable video generation, and contemporary video world models, and identifies where each line either declines or fails to support queryable behaviour. §3 develops the compression view in concrete terms: what is compressed, how the latent is structured, and what queries the architecture is required to support. §4 describes the model and the two-stage training. §5 reports experiments along three axes — random-access frame and depth readout, cross-query consistency on shared scenes, and standard controllable generation as a sanity baseline — chosen to make the case that queryability is a substantive capability rather than relabelled control. §6 discusses what the framing leaves open, in particular action-conditioned and language-conditioned queries, and what would be required to add them without breaking the rest.
